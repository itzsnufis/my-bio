os/

An operating system, from an empty file

active · boots to a shell
the problem

Every abstraction I leaned on daily was somebody else's decision. Page tables, scheduling, interrupt handling — all of it worked, none of it was mine to explain. That is a strange place to stand as someone who calls themselves a systems developer, so the abstraction had to go. Started at the boot sector.

the approach

Small steps, each with a visible checkpoint: text mode, then a framebuffer, then an IDT that survives deliberately triggered exceptions, then a physical allocator, then paging, then a cooperative scheduler, then preemption. Every stage had to survive a cold boot on real hardware, because "works after a soft reset" is not a result.

The honest part: the scheduler has been rewritten twice, and the second version is only now better than the first. Interrupt latency taught me to stop trusting intuition and go back to reading the manual properly.

current state

Boots on x86-64 into a shell that runs a handful of real commands. Userspace isolation exists and is not trusted yet. Next: clean up the process model and get the filesystem past "suspiciously optimistic".

phone/

A phone that opens with a screwdriver

bring-up · on the bench
the problem

A phone is the computer most people trust the least and understand the most rarely: sealed shut, undocumented at the edges, impossible to repair without a service manual nobody handed out. I wanted one where every wire is accounted for and every layer can be replaced.

the approach

A Raspberry Pi as the compute heart, mounted on a prototype carrier board. Around it, the parts that separate a computer from a phone: a real power path with battery, charger and regulators, a display over DSI, audio, a modem, and the glue logic that lets all of that talk to a board designed for none of it.

That glue is where the work actually sits — level shifting, bus arbitration on I2C, power sequencing so nothing browns out when the radio wakes up, and thermal management that openly admits a Pi in a pocket-sized case is an ambitious machine.

Software-wise it runs on a cut-down Linux for now. The operating system project is the long game: every subsystem a phone needs is one I eventually want to own, so the two projects keep trading pieces.

current state

It boots, it drives the display, and it can place a call — which was the moment this stopped being a pile of boards. Currently losing a fight with antenna tuning, and relaying the carrier board for revision two, where hopefully all the cables point the right way.

rpi/

The Raspberry Pi bench

ongoing · always plugged in
the problem

Prototyping needs a place where boards, cables and bad ideas can meet without endangering the main machine. Mine is a corner of the desk with a permanently mounted Pi, a breadboard that never quite gets cleared, and a firm habit of measuring before guessing.

the approach

One experiment at a time, written down: bus timing, sensor bring-up, GPIO work, thermal profiling, and small utilities that turn a Pi into a passable lab instrument — a serial logger here, a crude protocol sniffer there. Anything that proves useful gets pulled forward into the phone project, which is the only reason the breadboard is allowed to stay messy.

current state

Rotating: SPI throughput on the Pico, behaviour under sustained thermal load, and a level-shifting circuit destined for the phone's carrier board. Half of these end with "that was obvious in hindsight", which is a fine outcome for a bench session.

tools/

Backend tooling with no surprises

running unattended
the problem

The interesting parts of a project get all the attention; the boring parts are what keep it alive at three in the morning. Builds, deployments, backups, log parsing, and the data plumbing nobody writes a blog post about.

the approach

Small Go binaries, statically linked, deployed as a single file. Python for anything that has to be written in one afternoon and understood the next day. SQL schemas designed before the first insert, because migrations are where optimism goes to die.

systemd units with explicit restart policies instead of a supervisor of the week, structured logs in one format so that grep remains a legitimate debugging strategy, and health checks that fail loudly enough to be noticed by a human, not just by a dashboard.

current state

Running unattended for months at a time. The work left is unglamorous and therefore being done: better failure messages, retention that does not silently fill a disk, and tests around the parts that most deserve to be forgotten.
